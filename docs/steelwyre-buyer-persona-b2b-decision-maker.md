## 2. Psychographics

**Identity:**
Marcus thinks of himself as a *strategist* forced to operate like a *project manager*. He got into marketing for the creative and analytical challenge. The reality: he spends 60–70% of his week coordinating execution — briefing freelancers, chasing approvals, pulling reports, updating decks for leadership.

**Values:**
- Efficiency over elegance (if it works, ship it)
- Data over gut feel (but will act on gut when data is slow)
- Ownership over outsourcing (wants control; hates being dependent on agencies)
- Results over process (will break internal process to hit a number)

**Self-perception:**
Smart, resourceful, slightly overextended. Proud of how much he gets done with a small team. Quietly frustrated he's not doing more strategic work. Follows Marketing Twitter/LinkedIn but rarely posts — too busy.

**Information diet:**
- Reads: Lenny's Newsletter, Demand Curve, Marketing Examples, SparkToro, MKT1
- Listens to: My First Million, Acquired, The Marketing Millennials, The CMO Podcast
- Community: Pavilion (or lurks), Growthmentor, Slack groups for his stack (HubSpot, GA4)
- Checks LinkedIn 3–4x/day, mostly for industry pulse and social proof validation

**Risk tolerance:**
Medium. Will try new tools in a 14-day trial before committing. Will not run a new tool past procurement if it costs more than $500/mo without a clear ROI story. Bootstrapped instincts even at a VC-backed company.

**Relationship to AI:**
Early adopter. Has a ChatGPT Plus subscription, uses Claude for drafts, maybe Perplexity for research. Has tried Jasper or Copy.ai. Got output that was "fine but generic." Frustrated that AI still requires him to do the heavy lifting. Knows AI is changing his job but unsure how to fully leverage it without a team to implement.

## 3. A Day in Their Life

**7:30am** — Opens Slack on phone before getting out of bed. Three messages from the CEO. One asking about last week's MQL number. One sharing a competitor's new campaign. One tagging him in a thread asking why LinkedIn CPC is up 18%.

**8:15am** — Coffee. Opens the dashboard. GA4 looks fine. HubSpot shows MQLs are down 12% WoW. Tries to figure out if it's a tracking issue or a real trend. Spends 25 minutes diagnosing. Real trend.

**9:00am** — Sprint planning with the team. Two writers are behind on content. The SEO contractor submitted a brief that needs revision. The PPC freelancer needs a revised ad creative by Friday.

**10:30am** — Reviews the email campaign that went out yesterday. Open rate: 26%. Click rate: 1.8%. Could be better. Rewrites the subject line retrospectively in his head. No time to document the learning.

**11:45am** — CEO calls. "Can we launch a campaign around the new feature next week?" Marcus makes mental math: content, landing page, email, paid ads, social. He says yes. He doesn't have the bandwidth to execute all of it properly but doesn't want to say no to a growth push.

**1:00pm** — Lunch at desk. Scrolls LinkedIn. Sees a competitor post getting 400 comments about their new product launch. Feels a low-grade dread he can't name.

**2:00pm** — Deep work block (often interrupted). Tries to write the Q2 strategy deck. Gets three Slack pings. Abandons the deck.

**3:30pm** — Fires up ChatGPT to draft the email campaign brief. Spends 40 min prompting, editing, re-prompting. Output is usable but needs significant polish. Sends to writer.

**5:00pm** — Updates the reporting dashboard. Numbers aren't where they need to be. Commits to "figuring out the strategy" this weekend. Doesn't.

**7:30pm** — Home. Still has 47 unread Slack messages. Checks email one more time. Plans to read the Demand Curve article he saved two weeks ago. Falls asleep before he gets to it.

**Net feeling:** Reactive, stretched, never doing the work that actually moves the needle. Knows what he should do — has no bandwidth to do it.

## 4. Top 3 Goals

### Goal 1: Prove marketing is working before the next funding round / board meeting
The clock is ticking. At a Series A company, the board wants to see CAC:LTV trending right and MQL-to-SQL pipeline healthy. Marcus needs his numbers to compound, not just grow linearly. His job security and personal brand are tied to whether this quarter's numbers support a growth narrative.

**What success looks like:** MQLs up 30%+ QoQ, CAC down, pipeline from marketing is traceable and defensible to the board.

### Goal 2: Get out of execution mode and into strategy mode
Every marketing leader Marcus follows talks about "owning the strategy." He's never gotten there. He wants to spend 60% of his time on positioning, channel experiments, messaging tests, and creative strategy — and delegate execution to a system he trusts. Not a freelancer. A system.

**What success looks like:** His week has calendar blocks for strategic thinking that don't get crushed by execution emergencies.

### Goal 3: Scale output without scaling headcount
The CEO has frozen headcount. Marcus needs to do more with the same 2.5-person team. He's already tried hiring offshore content writers (quality inconsistent), buying tools that half-work, and consolidating his stack. Nothing has solved the throughput problem cleanly.

**What success looks like:** Doubling campaign output — more tests, more channels, more touchpoints — without hiring a fourth full-time person.

## 5. Top 3 Pain Points

### Pain Point 1: The strategy-execution chasm (visceral daily pain)
Marcus can articulate exactly what the Q2 campaign should look like. He knows the messaging angle, the channels, the audience segments, the cadence. He could write the brief in 30 minutes. What he cannot do is make it real. By the time the brief is written, reviewed, sent to writers, revised, sent to design, approved, loaded into the email tool, the landing page built, the paid campaigns set up — three weeks have passed and the moment has softened. He has a near-perfect strategy that executed late and underperformed. Then he has to explain why in the next board update.

The gap is not intelligence or strategy. The gap is a machine that converts strategy to execution at speed.

### Pain Point 2: The freelancer-dependency spiral (operational pain)
His content writer needs a brief. His SEO contractor needs keywords. His PPC freelancer needs ad copy. His designer needs copy before making assets. All of it flows through Marcus. He is the bottleneck in his own system. He has tried project management tools (Asana, Linear, Notion). The tools don't execute — they just make the queue more visible. Three freelancer relationships means three channels for things to go wrong. And when a freelancer drops out, he absorbs their work personally for two to four weeks.

### Pain Point 3: AI tools that generate, not do (category frustration)
Marcus has churned from two or three AI tools. The pattern: demos look impressive, trial feels generic, workflow still requires him to do the real work. He pays for Jasper or Copy.ai, gets decent first drafts, still has to edit everything, still has to publish it, still has to set up the campaign, still has to QA the assets. The tool saved him 40 minutes per deliverable but didn't save him the 20 hours of orchestration per campaign. He has started calling AI tools "fancy autocomplete." He says this slightly angrily. It's because he expected more and didn't get it.

He does not want a writing tool. He wants a machine that receives a campaign goal and returns a running campaign.

## 6. Buying Process / Decision Journey

### Stage 1: Problem recognition (weeks 1–4)
Triggered by one of: a missed pipeline target, a competitive threat from a better-resourced rival, a board conversation about marketing ROI, or a key freelancer departing. Marcus starts searching for solutions that feel systemic, not tactical.

Search behavior: "AI marketing automation," "marketing execution tools," "replace marketing agency AI," "marketing automation for small teams."

Content consumed: blog posts comparing tools, Reddit threads in r/marketing and r/SaaS, LinkedIn posts from founders sharing AI marketing experiments.

### Stage 2: Category education (weeks 2–6)
Marcus discovers he is not looking for a content tool or a CRM — he's looking for something he doesn't have a clean name for. "AI marketing execution" starts to crystallize as the category. He bookmarks 5–8 tools. Reads comparison articles. Watches one or two demo videos.

Key decision criteria forming:
- Does it actually publish/run campaigns, or just generate?
- Does it integrate with his existing stack (HubSpot, Google Ads, Meta)?
- Is pricing sane for a team of 3 (not enterprise)?
- Is there a trial with real output?

### Stage 3: Shortlist and demo (weeks 4–8)
Shortlists 2–3 tools. SteelWyre makes this list if: (a) positioning clearly differentiates from "just AI writing," (b) integration with his stack is visible on the website, (c) pricing is transparent and under $500/mo at entry.

Books a demo or starts a free trial. In the trial, he runs one real campaign — not a toy example. If the output is close to what he'd brief a team to produce, and if it publishes or sets up correctly in his tools, he is extremely interested.

Critical moment: He shows it to the CEO or a trusted peer. If their reaction is "this is real," he moves.

### Stage 4: Internal approval (week 6–10)
At a 15–60 person company, Marcus can often approve tools under $500/mo without a procurement cycle. Over $500/mo he needs CEO or CFO signoff. He needs:
- Clear ROI story ("replaces 1 contractor = saves $3K/mo")
- Proof it works for his company type (case study from a company like his)
- Confidence that if it fails, it was a reasonable bet (covers his downside)

He does not want a sales call at this stage. He wants an async response to 2–3 specific questions.

### Stage 5: Decision (week 8–12)
Buys when: first-run campaign looks like something he'd have briefed himself (or better), the platform integrated without drama, and he can see a clear 30-day ROI milestone.

Kills the deal when: setup is complex, output needs heavy editing, or he detects platform instability (bugs, slow support, unclear roadmap).

## 7. Key Objections to SteelWyre

### Objection 1: "AI can't understand my brand/voice/audience"
**Root fear:** He'll publish something off-brand that embarrasses him or confuses prospects. He's seen generic AI output and doesn't trust it near customer-facing assets.

**Real counter:** SteelWyre learns from his existing assets, not from scratch. The question to neutralize this: "What if SteelWyre trained on your top 10 campaigns and matched that voice?"

### Objection 2: "I'll still have to babysit it"
**Root fear:** Every tool promises autonomy and delivers another thing to manage. He's been burned. The Jasper scar is fresh.

**Real counter:** Show him — in 10 minutes of demo — an actual campaign built and launched with two inputs. Not a walkthrough. A live run. The proof is in the doing, not the describing.

### Objection 3: "What happens when it makes a mistake in a paid campaign?"
**Root fear:** A bad AI decision on Google Ads or Meta Ads could burn budget or trigger policy violations. He is accountable for this. His job requires him not to have "the AI did it" as an excuse.

**Real counter:** Position the guardrails front and center. "You set the budget, the creative limits, the targeting parameters. SteelWyre operates inside those limits. It doesn't exceed your permissions." This converts the objection from AI risk to delegation clarity.

### Objection 4: "We're already paying for HubSpot. Does SteelWyre replace it or add to it?"
**Root fear:** Stack bloat. He already has tools he's not using fully. Another tool that doesn't integrate cleanly with HubSpot is a non-starter.

**Real counter:** SteelWyre works with HubSpot, not against it. "HubSpot stores your data. SteelWyre acts on it." Integration is a feature, not a workaround.

### Objection 5: "We can't afford downtime if this breaks mid-campaign"
**Root fear:** Accountability risk. He is responsible to his CEO for campaign performance. A tool failure during a live campaign is a career moment.

**Real counter:** Offer a phased onboarding — run SteelWyre in parallel with one campaign before fully migrating. Reduce the perceived cliff. Also: make support response time a visible commitment on the pricing page.

- **A peer referral** — Another head of marketing in his network saying "we've been using it for 3 months, here's what changed." This is worth 10 sales calls.

## 11. Anti-Persona: Who Is NOT a Good Fit

### Do NOT target these profiles with SteelWyre's current positioning:

**The Enterprise Marketing Manager (100+ person company)**
Has a dedicated ops team, a martech stack managed by RevOps, a procurement process, and a legal team that reviews vendor contracts. Needs SAML SSO, SOC 2, custom SLAs, MSA negotiations. SteelWyre is not built for this motion and will lose to HubSpot, Salesforce, or Marketo in every conversation. Time and energy cost: high. Win rate: near zero.

**The Solopreneur / One-Person Business**
Already using ChatGPT for free. Will not pay $299/mo for campaign automation when they run one campaign per quarter. Has no stack to integrate with. Low LTV, high support cost. This is the person SteelWyre has to be careful not to attract via broad "AI for marketing" messaging — they'll sign up, not activate, and churn at day 14. They distort conversion data.

**The "AI Skeptic" Operations-First Owner**
Runs a brick-and-mortar or service business where marketing is handled by a nephew or a $500/mo social media manager. Has never heard of HubSpot. Does not think in funnels. Is not suffering from execution bandwidth — they are suffering from marketing knowledge. SteelWyre assumes a baseline of marketing sophistication. Without it, the tool creates confusion, not value.

**The Agency Owner Trying to Replace Strategy With AI**
An agency principal who wants SteelWyre to do the thinking for the client, not just the execution. SteelWyre can accelerate execution for an agency that already owns strategy. But if the agency's value proposition to clients is "we think for you," and they want SteelWyre to do that thinking — they're likely to underdeliver client expectations and blame the tool. Good for execution-layer agency use; bad for strategy-layer replacement.

**The Bootstrapped Two-Person Startup With No Marketing Foundation**
No brand voice documented, no existing content, no customer data, no integrated ad accounts. SteelWyre needs fuel to run. If the inputs are thin, the outputs will be thin. This persona signs up hopeful, struggles through onboarding, and churns frustrated. Not because SteelWyre failed — because the prerequisites weren't there. Requires a different product motion (more guided setup) before being a good fit.

## Strategic Implications for SteelWyre

**Positioning must speak to execution, not content generation.**
Every message that sounds like "AI writes your marketing copy" repels Marcus. He's tried it. He's disappointed. The message that lands is "AI runs your campaigns" — specific, outcome-oriented, distinguished from everything he's already churned.

**The trial must produce a real campaign in the first session.**
If Marcus opens the trial and sees a blank workspace or a prompt to "upload your brand assets," he closes it. The onboarding must get to a working campaign output — even a partial one — in the first 15 minutes. This is the activation event. Everything else is table stakes.

**Social proof must be specific and role-matched.**
"Trusted by 2,000+ marketers" means nothing to Marcus. "Rachel Kim, Head of Marketing at [B2B SaaS Company], 40 employees, Series A — reduced contractor spend by $3,800/mo while increasing campaign output by 4x" is the kind of testimonial that stops him scrolling.

**The ~18-month window is real from his perspective too.**
Marcus knows OpenAI and Anthropic are building workflow automation. He's read the articles. He is actively looking for a tool that is purpose-built for his problem before the generic platforms commoditize "AI marketing." SteelWyre should acknowledge this tension in its messaging — being the specialized, marketing-native tool is the moat, and Marcus understands specialized tools vs. generic ones.

**Agencies are a viable secondary market but require a different message.**
For the agency variant of this persona, the value prop shifts from "replace your contractors" to "increase margin per client without hiring." Same execution gap, different financial framing. Consider a separate landing page and case study track for the agency segment.
